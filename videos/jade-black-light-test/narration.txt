Last spring, a woman came into my shop carrying a green bangle wrapped in a dish towel. Her father bought it in Hong Kong in 1991, on an anniversary trip, for her mother. It came with a little certificate, folded in four, that said natural jadeite. Grade A. Untreated. Her mother wore it every day for thirty years. Now she wanted to give it to her own daughter, and she wanted to know one thing first. Is it real?

I did not reach for my loupe. I did not reach for my scale. I turned off the lights, took a two-dollar black light out of my coat pocket, and shined it on that bangle.

It lit up like a glow stick.

Here is the truth nobody behind a jewelry counter will say out loud. That glow, by itself, proves nothing. But it is the fastest question you can ask a piece of jade, and the old dealers ask it first, before a single dollar is discussed. Stay with me. By the end of this, you will know exactly what that glow means, what it does not mean, the one mistake that makes people trust a fake, and the three quiet checks the old dealers did the moment the lights came back on.

I learned this from a man named Leo. He ran a small counter on Forty-Seventh Street when I was young, and he had a strange history for a New York jeweler. In 1939, Leo was a twelve-year-old boy from Vienna. His family found passage to the one great city that would still take Jews without a visa. Shanghai. Somewhere between seventeen and twenty thousand Jewish refugees ended up there, most of them crowded into a poor district called Hongkew.

Leo's father found work sweeping floors for a Chinese jade merchant. And that merchant had one rule. Nobody talks price until the stone has been seen in every kind of light. Daylight. Lamplight. And the dark of his back room, under an ultraviolet lamp that showed him glued repairs and painted color.

When Leo came to New York after the war, he brought that habit with him. Every estate lot, every grandmother's bangle, every little carved Buddha that crossed his counter went into the dark first. Forty years later, a new kind of fake flooded the market, and that old habit turned out to be worth a fortune.

Now, is this a secret only Jewish dealers knew? No. Every serious jade dealer from Hong Kong to Manhattan owns one of these lights. But the habit of asking the dark before you ask the price, that is what the old men on Forty-Seventh Street drilled into us.

So first, you need to know what you are actually testing for, because most people have no idea what the word jade even means.

The name comes from Spanish. Piedra de ijada. The stone of the side. Spanish explorers in the Americas saw people pressing it against their bodies to ease the pain in their side. Then, in 1863, a French mineralogist named Alexis Damour showed that jade is not one stone at all. It is two. Jadeite, the harder, glassier, brighter one, which comes mostly from Burma, today Myanmar. And nephrite, the oilier, softer-looking one, the jade of ancient Chinese carving. Both are real jade. If a lab ever tells you yours is nephrite, nobody cheated you.

Here is what people do get cheated with. Glass, poured into molds. Quartz, dyed green and sold under names like Malaysia jade. Serpentine, a soft green stone sold as new jade. And then the big one. Real jadeite that has been doctored.

The trade sorts jadeite with letters. A jade is natural. Nothing added except, maybe, a thin coat of wax on the surface, which is normal and accepted. B jade is the one that changed everything. Around the 1980s, workshops began soaking cheap, brownish jadeite in acid for days, sometimes weeks, to eat out the stains. That left the stone full of tiny open channels, so they pumped it full of plastic resin to make it look clear and glassy. C jade is dyed. And B plus C is all three. Bleached, filled, and dyed.

Here is why that matters. B jade is still jadeite. It passes the cold test. It passes the scratch test. It weighs almost exactly what the real thing weighs. But the acid leaves it brittle, the resin can yellow over years of sun and heat, and dealers who know what they are doing pay a small fraction of what natural jade brings. Some put it at five to ten percent.

Your checklist. One. Jadeite and nephrite are both real jade. Two. Glass, dyed quartz, and serpentine are the imitators. Three. The most dangerous fake is real jadeite filled with plastic, and that is exactly the fake the black light was made to catch.

So why does the light work at all? A black light sends out ultraviolet, a color your eye cannot see. The minerals in natural jade mostly swallow it and stay quiet. In a dark room, untreated jadeite usually looks dim and a little purple, because all you are seeing is the light bouncing off its polish.

But many of the resins and dyes are different. They soak up that invisible ultraviolet and throw it back out as light you can see. That is called fluorescence. It is the same reason a white shirt glows at a bowling alley, and the same reason a glass of tonic water turns bright blue under a black light.

So when that bangle lit up in my shop, I was not looking at the jade. I was looking at the plastic inside the jade.

And the shape of the glow tells you something too. Gemologists at the Gemological Institute of America have noted that resin tends to glow fairly evenly across the whole piece, because it filled the whole piece. Dye tends to glow only where the dye sits. In the green patches. In the veins. In the cracks.

Your checklist. One. Natural jade usually stays dark or barely glows. Two. An even glow across the whole piece points to resin. Three. A glow that follows the color points to dye.

Now, the light itself. The one in my coat pocket is a keychain light. It cost about two dollars. You will find them online and at hardware stores, sold for finding pet stains on carpet.

But here is the detail almost every video skips. Black lights come in two common kinds, and the package will tell you which. Three-ninety-five nanometers, and three-sixty-five nanometers. The cheap three-ninety-five lights leak a lot of ordinary purple light, and that purple wash can hide a weak glow. A three-sixty-five light looks dimmer to your eye, but it makes stones fluoresce much more clearly. If you can find a three-sixty-five, buy that one. If all you have is a three-ninety-five, it will still catch a strong glow, and the strong glow is the one that matters most.

Two warnings. Never shine it into anyone's eyes, including your own, and keep it away from children's faces. Ultraviolet damage builds up where you cannot feel it. A pair of clear safety glasses marked for UV protection costs a few dollars, and that is money well spent.

And before you test any jade, test the light. Hold it over a twenty-dollar bill. The thin security strip inside will glow green. If it glows, your light is working.

Your checklist. One. Look for three-sixty-five on the package. Two. Never in the eyes. Three. Check the light on a twenty-dollar bill before you trust it.

Here is exactly how Leo taught me to do it.

Go into the darkest room in your house. A bathroom with no window, or a closet. Close the door, and give your eyes one full minute to adjust. That minute matters. A faint glow is invisible to eyes that just walked in from a bright kitchen.

Lay the piece on a dark cloth. Not a white towel. White fabric glows under ultraviolet from the brighteners in laundry soap, and it will fool you.

Hold the light a few inches away and move it slowly over every part of the piece. The front. The back. The inside of a bangle. The drill hole of a pendant. And if you have more than one piece of jade, lay them side by side. Your eye needs a comparison. Next to its neighbors, a filled piece almost always gives itself away.

Then turn the lights back on and look again in daylight, so you know exactly where the color sits.

Your checklist. One. A dark room and one full minute for your eyes. Two. A dark cloth underneath, never white. Three. Compare pieces side by side.

Now, what you see will fall into one of three results.

Result one. A strong, even glow across the whole piece. Usually a chalky blue-white, sometimes greenish or yellowish. Like a glow stick. That is the red flag. That piece has very likely been filled with resin. Do not pay a natural jade price for it, and if you just bought it as untreated, ask for your money back.

Result two. A glow that lights up only in streaks or patches, usually right where the brightest color sits. That points to dye.

Result three. The piece stays dark, or shows only a faint, soft haze. And this is the mistake that makes people trust a fake. A dark result is not a clean bill of health.

Two reasons. First, natural jade is often finished with a thin coat of wax, and wax can give a faint glow of its own. So a whisper of light is normal. Second, and this is the one that keeps honest dealers up at night, the people who make treated jade know about the black light too. Some newer resins barely glow at all. One gem dealer sent a bangle to a major lab after it stayed completely dark under his light. The report came back. Filled with polymer, and dyed.

So here is how Leo used to say it. A strong glow is a no. A dark stone is a maybe. It is never a yes.

Your checklist. One. A strong, even glow, assume resin. Two. A glow that follows the color, assume dye. Three. Dark only means keep testing.

That is why the old dealers used the light first, not only. Here are the three things Leo did the moment the lights came back on.

The loupe. A ten-power magnifier and a bright light. Natural jade shows a felted texture, tiny grains locked together. Glass shows round gas bubbles and swirl lines, and no stone on Earth grows perfectly round bubbles. Dye shows up as a green spider web, darker in the tiny cracks between the grains, paler in between. And filled jade, under a light held low across the surface, often shows a fine network of etched lines, like the skin of an orange.

The weight. This is the one I trust most at home. Jadeite is heavy for its size, about three and a third times heavier than the same amount of water. Nephrite, about three. Most glass, quartz, and serpentine come in around two and a half to two and three quarters. Weigh the piece dry on a kitchen scale that reads tenths of a gram. Then set a cup of water on the scale, press zero, and hang the piece on a thread so it sits fully under the water without touching the cup. Divide the dry weight by the number on the scale. Around three point three, you are in jadeite country. Under two point eight, it is not jadeite. This will not catch B jade, because it still weighs like jadeite. But it knocks out every imitator at once. Loose pieces only. Nothing set in gold or silver.

The tap. Hang a bangle on a string and tap it gently with a coin. A solid, natural bangle rings clear and long. A filled bangle gives a short, dull clack, because the resin swallows the vibration. Careful, though. Quartz rings beautifully too, and a cracked natural bangle thuds. And tap over a bed, never over a tile floor.

Your checklist. One. The loupe, for bubbles and dye webs. Two. The weight, to knock out glass, quartz, and serpentine. Three. The tap, for filled bangles.

Now, what you must never do. Never hold a lighter to your jade. That old trick with a hair wrapped around the bangle is how people scorch a piece that has plastic inside it. Never drag a knife across the face of a carving. Never use acid, boiling water, or the ultrasonic tank at the jewelry counter on a piece you are unsure of. Filled jade does not forgive heat or harsh chemicals. And if you watched my video on cleaning jewelry with ammonia, keep your jade out of that bowl. Warm water, a drop of mild soap, a soft cloth. That is all jade ever needs.

Now let me tell you why any of this is worth your time. In 2014, a single strand of twenty-seven jadeite beads, made by Cartier in 1933 as a wedding gift for the American heiress Barbara Hutton, sold at Sotheby's in Hong Kong for about twenty-seven and a half million dollars. The buyer was Cartier itself. At the time, it was a world auction record for jadeite jewelry.

That is the top of the market. Fine, bright, translucent green jadeite is one of the most expensive gems on Earth. So when a shop on vacation offers you a glowing green bangle with a certificate for the price of dinner, the certificate is part of the costume.

A report is only as good as the lab that wrote it, and only if it belongs to the stone in your hand. For jade, the names that mean something are labs like the Gemological Institute of America and the Hong Kong Jade and Stone Laboratory. Call the lab. Look up the report number. And check the weight and measurements on the paper against your own kitchen scale. Forgers get lazy with the numbers.

And when do you stop testing and pay a lab? If the piece passed the light, the loupe, and the weight, and you plan to sell it, insure it, or divide it between your children, send it in. Their infrared instruments see resin and dye that no flashlight ever will. And get the report before you sell, not after somebody makes you an offer.

Your checklist. One. A cheap, bright green bangle is never what it claims. Two. Verify every certificate with the lab itself. Three. For anything you will sell or insure, the lab has the last word.
